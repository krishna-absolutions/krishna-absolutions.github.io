Knowledge Hub · Loan Basics

Types of home loans, explained plainly

The choices that actually change your repayments and flexibility — laid out without the jargon.

Fixed rate vs. variable rate

Fixed rate

Your interest rate is locked for an agreed period (commonly 1–5 years). Repayments stay the same regardless of what happens to market rates — useful for budgeting certainty, but you may face break costs if you exit early, and you generally can't access unlimited extra repayments.

Variable rate

Your rate moves with the market, up or down. You get more flexibility — unlimited extra repayments, offset accounts, redraw — but your repayment amount can change over the life of the loan.

Many borrowers split the difference with a split loan — part fixed, part variable — to get some certainty and some flexibility at the same time.

Interest-only vs. principal & interest

Interest-only (I/O)

Your repayments only cover the interest charged, for an agreed period (typically up to 5 years). Repayments are lower during that period, but you're not reducing the loan balance — common for investment properties where cash flow and tax deductibility matter.

Principal & interest (P&I)

Each repayment reduces both the interest owed and the loan balance itself. Repayments are higher than interest-only, but you're actually building equity and paying the loan down — the standard approach for most owner-occupier loans.

Offset account vs. redraw facility

Offset account

A linked everyday bank account. The balance in it is "offset" against your loan balance before interest is calculated — so $10,000 sitting in offset against a $500,000 loan means you only pay interest on $490,000. Funds stay fully accessible, like a normal transaction account.

Redraw facility

Lets you withdraw extra repayments you've already made above the minimum. It reduces interest the same way an offset does, but the funds are part of the loan itself, not a separate account — some lenders restrict redraw amounts or charge a small fee per withdrawal.

Guarantor loans

A family member (typically a parent) offers equity in their own property as additional security, which can let you borrow with a smaller deposit — sometimes without paying Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI) at all. The guarantor takes on real risk, so this is a decision that needs its own careful conversation with everyone involved before proceeding.

Construction loans

Funds are released to the builder in stages as the build progresses (slab, frame, lock-up, fit-out, completion), rather than as one lump sum at settlement. You generally only pay interest on the amount drawn down so far, not the full approved loan amount, which keeps repayments lower during the build.

Which one is right for you?

There's no universally "best" option — it depends on your income stability, whether the property is for you to live in or rent out, how much deposit you have, and how much flexibility you value against certainty. That's exactly the conversation a strategy call is for.
